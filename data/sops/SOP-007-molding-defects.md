# Purpose and scope

Diagnosis of moulding defects logged against M-11 to M-14: SINK_MARK,
SHORT_SHOT, FLASH, WARP and CONTAMINATION. Change one variable at a time and
record the result; changing three at once teaches you nothing.

# SINK_MARK

Depressions on the surface opposite a thick section. The most frequently
logged moulding defect on this line.

Check in order:

1. Holding pressure too low or holding time too short. Increase holding time
   first; it is the safer adjustment.
2. Melt temperature too high, giving excessive shrinkage on cooling.
3. Gate freezing before the part is packed out. A gate that is undersized
   for the wall section will sink no matter what the process does.
4. Wet resin. Confirm the dryer ran the full cycle.

# SHORT_SHOT

Incomplete fill. Usually the cavity furthest from the gate.

1. Check for a starved feed: hopper level, feed throat bridging, screw
   recovery time.
2. Injection pressure or velocity too low.
3. Melt or mold temperature too low.
4. Blocked vents. Trapped air will stop the flow front short.

# FLASH

Material escaping the parting line or around ejector pins.

1. Clamp tonnage too low for the projected area. Verify against the mold tag.
2. Uneven clamp torque after a changeover. Re-torque in a cross pattern.
3. Parting line damage or debris.
4. Melt temperature or injection pressure too high.

# WARP

Distortion after ejection.

1. Uneven cooling. Check that all water circuits are connected and flowing;
   a capped-off circuit after a changeover is a common cause.
2. Ejection too early. Increase cooling time.
3. Non-uniform packing across cavities.

# CONTAMINATION

Foreign material or colour streaking in the part.

1. Incomplete purge after a colour or material change. Purge until clean.
2. Regrind ratio too high or contaminated regrind.
3. Hopper or throat not vacuumed at changeover.
4. Degraded material from an extended residence time at temperature.

# When to stop and escalate

If a defect persists after two documented process adjustments, stop and
escalate. Repeated small adjustments across a shift produce a process nobody
can reproduce tomorrow.
